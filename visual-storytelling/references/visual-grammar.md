# Visual Grammar

Use this reference to turn a semantic relationship into a compact visual system.
Select the simplest grammar that preserves the idea. Combine grammars only when
the combination creates meaning that neither can carry alone.

## Metaphor Families

### Contrast and comparison

Use parallel objects, divided fields, mirrored positions, or matched scales to
show a meaningful difference.

- Best for: before/after, stated/actual, cost/value, breadth/depth,
  conventional/alternative.
- Useful primitives: split field, two bars, two paths, matched containers,
  foreground/background.
- Keep constant: everything except the dimension under comparison.
- Common failure: exaggerating multiple differences until the viewer cannot
  identify the claimed cause.

### Accumulation and compounding

Use repeated units, growing stacks, lengthening shadows, or changing slopes to
make small actions visibly aggregate.

- Best for: habits, learning, debt, trust, distribution, technical debt.
- Useful primitives: dots, bricks, steps, bars, rings, repeated marks.
- Show the mechanism, not merely a generic arrow pointing upward.
- Common failure: implying exponential growth when the source supports only
  steady accumulation.

### Filter and perception

Place an intervening surface between source and result. Change, omit, bend, or
relabel what passes through it.

- Best for: bias, translation, incentives, interfaces, measurement, mediation.
- Useful primitives: lens, aperture, sieve, prism, shadow, mask, window.
- Make the input and output comparable enough that the transformation reads.
- Common failure: using a filter as decoration without specifying what changes.

### Boundary and constraint

Use walls, frames, gates, containers, narrow passages, or cropped fields to show
what limits or directs behavior.

- Best for: focus, rules, scarcity, safety, specialization, positioning.
- Useful primitives: box, fence, doorway, lane, mold, spotlight.
- Distinguish a productive constraint from confinement through the resulting
  direction or energy.
- Common failure: treating all boundaries as negative.

### Leverage and asymmetry

Show a small input creating, moving, or controlling a disproportionate output.

- Best for: tools, code, media, capital, delegation, distribution.
- Useful primitives: lever, pulley, switch, fulcrum, branching network, domino.
- Make the mechanism of amplification visible.
- Common failure: showing a size difference without showing why it exists.

### Selection and focus

Reduce a field of possibilities to one chosen element, path, or illuminated
region.

- Best for: priorities, attention, curation, positioning, decisions.
- Useful primitives: crop, beam, target, funnel, one marked node, erased paths.
- Show the opportunity cost or gained clarity when it is central to the idea.
- Common failure: confusing visibility with importance.

### Sequence and progress

Arrange states along a path, ladder, curve, track, or repeated interval.

- Best for: learning, maturity, process, effort, delay, path dependence.
- Useful primitives: steps, milestones, trail, rungs, frames, changing gaps.
- Encode what changes at each stage rather than adding arbitrary checkpoints.
- Show the counterforce when resistance, friction, or a remaining obstacle is
  what makes progress meaningful.
- Common failure: implying a smooth journey when setbacks are the point.

### Feedback and cycles

Return output to input through a loop whose direction and effect are visible.

- Best for: habits, markets, algorithms, trust, incentives, flywheels.
- Useful primitives: circular path, reciprocal arrows, growing orbit, echo.
- Label the reinforcing or balancing effect, not every node.
- Common failure: drawing a loop for a process that does not actually recur.

### Hierarchy and dependency

Use nesting, support, roots, stacks, or branching to show what contains, enables,
or depends on what.

- Best for: systems, strategy, infrastructure, causes, organizational models.
- Useful primitives: pyramid, tree, nested frame, foundation, suspended load.
- Make the direction of dependency unambiguous.
- Common failure: making vertical position look like moral or numerical rank.

### Absence and negative space

Let a missing piece, gap, silhouette, interrupted pattern, or unoccupied region
carry the message.

- Best for: omission, opportunity, loneliness, optionality, invisible work,
  missing context.
- Useful primitives: gap, cutout, broken rhythm, empty chair, unfilled cell.
- Give the absence a clear relationship to the surrounding system.
- Common failure: mistaking emptiness for meaning.

### Material behavior

Map the idea onto a familiar physical behavior such as weight, friction,
pressure, tension, erosion, reflection, or shadow.

- Best for: emotional and systemic forces that are difficult to depict directly.
- Useful primitives: stretched line, compressed spring, cracked surface, magnet,
  ice, smoke, gravity.
- Preserve the rules of the chosen material enough to make the analogy credible.
- Common failure: mixing physical logics in one frame.

### Symbolic inversion

Start with a sign whose conventional operation is already understood, then
reverse, open, rotate, separate, or reorient it so the transformation expresses
the subject's thesis.

- Best for: identity systems, logos, recurring motifs, product narratives.
- Useful primitives: brackets, doors, arrows, containers, switches, cursors,
  frames.
- Preserve enough of the original sign that the inversion reads instantly.
- Test three layers: structural recognition, conceptual alignment, and
  deployment across sizes and contexts.
- Common failure: inventing a clever explanation after drawing an arbitrary
  mark.

## Composition System

### Canvas

- Choose the final aspect ratio before composing.
- Favor a centered or deliberately off-center dominant relationship.
- Establish generous outer margins and a repeatable spacing unit.
- Test at the smallest likely display size.

### Contrast

- Begin with black, white, and tonal separation.
- Use an accent as information, not atmosphere.
- Keep the focal element's contrast distinct from supporting structure.
- Check that meaning survives grayscale unless color itself is the subject.

### Shape

- Use the fewest shape families that can explain the relationship.
- Repeat shape language consistently across a sequence.
- Prefer precise silhouette and spacing over ornamental detail.
- Keep icons generic enough to be instantly recognized and specific enough to
  avoid ambiguity.

### Type

- Use one strong hierarchy with few sizes and weights.
- Keep labels close to the objects they identify.
- Avoid paragraphs inside the frame.
- Treat line breaks as pacing and meaning, not automatic wrapping.
- Proof exact spelling, attribution, and punctuation in the rendered artifact.

### Motion

- Animate one semantic change at a time.
- Hold long enough for the starting state to register.
- Use movement to reveal causality, not to decorate a static idea.
- End on a stable frame that can stand alone as the conclusion.

## TRAIN Pass

Use Visualize Value's public TRAIN framework as a final design audit:

- **Typography:** make type choice, weight, scale, and spacing carry deliberate
  meaning.
- **Restraint:** choose the palette, type system, and layout rules once so the
  idea receives the creative attention.
- **Alignment:** make invisible relationships feel intentional through a grid.
- **Image treatment:** crop, filter, frame, and render images consistently across
  the system.
- **Negative space:** use empty space to frame, separate, pause, or represent
  absence.

TRAIN is a constraint system, not a substitute for a concept. A perfectly
aligned visual can still say nothing.

## Candidate Score

Score each candidate from 0 to 2 on five dimensions:

| Dimension | 0 | 1 | 2 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Immediate clarity | Relationship is opaque | Reads with explanation | Reads at a glance |
| Fidelity | Distorts the idea | Preserves most meaning | Preserves the load-bearing claim |
| Compression | Needs many elements | Has minor excess | Nothing nonessential remains |
| Depth | Only illustrates nouns | Adds a modest reframe | Rewards a second look |
| Originality | Reconstructs a known visual | Uses a familiar trope plainly | Makes a fresh, subject-specific move |

Reject any candidate scoring 0 on clarity, fidelity, or originality. Prefer the
highest total only after those gates pass.

## Source Basis

These principles derive from observable, publicly described mechanisms rather
than copied artworks:

- Visualize Value describes making intangible services tangible, using
  recognizable symbols, maintaining strict visual constraints, and using
  hierarchy to widen comprehension:
  <https://visualizevalue.com/blogs/feed/how-to-create-visual-leverage>
- Jack Butcher describes Visualize Value as a daily practice of distilling ideas
  through a specific set of visual constraints:
  <https://www.jack.art/work/visualize-value>
- The official project archive demonstrates repeated use of high-contrast,
  sparse conceptual relationships across many subjects:
  <https://visualizevalue.com/visuals>
- The public RoboStrategy case study demonstrates symbolic inversion and tests
  one mark structurally, conceptually, and across deployed contexts:
  <https://visualizevalue.com/work/robostrategy-logo-brand>
- The open-source `vvriter` knowledge layer publishes the TRAIN framework and
  separates raw-material selection, voice, visuals, and output formats:
  <https://github.com/visualizevalue/vvriter>
- In a live Learning Leader visual exercise, Jack iterates on familiar progress
  imagery by removing unnecessary arrows and making the resisting force part of
  the explanation: <https://learningleader.com/butcherhawk379/>

Use these sources for provenance or refresh. Do not treat any individual work as
a template to reconstruct.
